## What Kit is for

Kit, formerly ConvertKit, is built around one job: helping people who make things (writers, podcasters, course creators, bloggers) turn an audience into a list, and a list into income. It isn't trying to be a general marketing suite, a CRM, or an ecommerce platform, and that narrow focus is the most useful thing to understand before you sign up. Every feature decision in the product traces back to "does this help a creator grow and monetize a list," and features outside that lane are either missing or clearly an afterthought.

That focus is also where most of the praise and most of the complaints in this review come from.

## What Kit gets right

### A free plan you can actually grow on

Kit's free plan covers up to 10,000 subscribers with unlimited landing pages, forms, and broadcast emails. That number is unusual. Most competitors cap their free tier at a few hundred contacts before pushing you toward a paid plan, which means a blogger who's still building an audience can hit a real, meaningful list size, thousands of people, without paying anything at all. If your bottleneck right now is growing the list rather than automating what happens after someone joins it, Kit's free tier gets out of your way longer than most.

### Tags instead of lists

Kit organizes subscribers around one pool with tags attached, rather than separate lists the way Mailchimp does. In practice, this means a subscriber who's interested in three different topics on your blog gets three tags and stays one person in your account, instead of three separate list memberships that can inflate your subscriber count and your bill. For anyone running more than one lead magnet or covering more than one topic, this is a simpler mental model, and it's the kind of decision that pays off quietly for years rather than showing up on a feature comparison chart.

### The automation and sequence builder, once you're paying for it

This is the part of Kit that earns its reputation. Sequences, Kit's term for automated email series, are well documented, visually laid out, and clearly the area the product team has put the most work into. Building a welcome sequence, delivering a lead magnet on autopilot, or tagging someone based on a link click is straightforward, and once you're on a paid plan, automations are unlimited with no step-count ceiling to bump into as your funnel gets more complex. A lot of tools nickel-and-dime you on automation steps as you scale; Kit doesn't, at least not past the point where you've already started paying.

### Deliverability that holds up

Inbox placement is hard to measure precisely from the outside, but Kit has built a solid reputation among creators specifically because its infrastructure and sending practices are tuned for regular content email rather than one-off marketing blasts. Bloggers who've switched from general-purpose marketing platforms often report fewer emails landing in promotions tabs or spam folders after the move. That's not a guarantee your specific emails will land perfectly, deliverability depends on your own sending habits too, but it's a real point in Kit's favor for a use case (recurring newsletter content) that some broader platforms weren't originally built around.

### Selling something without bolting on a second tool

Kit's Commerce features let creators sell digital products and paid subscriptions directly, without wiring up a separate checkout tool just to sell an ebook or a paid newsletter tier. It's not a full storefront platform, more on that below, but for a blogger whose "product" is a PDF, a template, or a paid version of the newsletter itself, having that live inside the same account you already use for email removes one more moving piece from your stack.

### Free migration help

Switching email platforms is tedious: rebuilding automations, re-importing subscribers, redoing tags or segments from scratch. Kit offers free migration assistance for people bringing a list over from another provider, and paid plans come with 24/7 email and chat support. Neither of those things shows up on a feature comparison chart, but both matter a lot the one time you need them.

## Where Kit falls short

### Zero automation on the free plan

This is the biggest asterisk on everything above. Kit's generous 10,000-subscriber free tier covers forms, landing pages, and broadcast emails, but automations are locked behind the paid Creator plan, which starts around $33 a month at 1,000 subscribers. That means a brand-new blogger with a small list still can't set up something as basic as an automated welcome email without paying, even though their subscriber count is nowhere near the free tier's ceiling. If a welcome sequence is the first thing you want to build, budget for the paid plan from day one rather than assuming the free tier gets you there.

### Pricing that climbs as your list grows

Kit's pricing scales with subscriber count, same as most competitors in this category. The Creator plan starts around $33 a month at 1,000 subscribers and increases in tiers as your list grows past that. That's a normal structure for this kind of tool, but it means a blogger with an actively growing list will keep watching the bill climb, and Kit isn't the cheapest option at every list size. Tools with flat pricing or a cheaper paid entry tier exist, and if budget is your main constraint rather than feature depth, it's worth checking those before committing.

### No real website, CRM, or ecommerce depth

Kit does one thing well and doesn't pretend to do the rest. There's no built-in website or landing-page builder in the way MailerLite offers one, no ecommerce storefront with a product catalog and inventory tracking the way a dedicated ecommerce platform would, and no CRM features like lead scoring or sales-pipeline tracking. Kit's Commerce tools handle a simple digital product or paid subscription fine, but they're not a Shopify or WooCommerce replacement, and if you're running an actual online store with inventory and order management, you'll need something else alongside Kit, or instead of it.

### Automation logic is simpler than dedicated marketing-automation tools

Kit's automation builder is good for what most bloggers need: welcome sequences, tag-based triggers, simple branching based on a link click or a form submission. It is not, however, built for the kind of deep conditional logic, lead scoring, or multi-team sales workflows you'd find in a platform like ActiveCampaign. If your plans involve elaborate multi-branch funnels that route people differently based on a dozen behavioral signals, you'll hit the edges of what Kit's visual builder is designed to do sooner than you would with a heavier automation-first tool.

### Reporting stays fairly basic

Kit gives you the numbers that matter for a content-focused list: open rates, click rates, subscriber growth, and which links people actually clicked. What it doesn't give you is the kind of deep attribution or revenue-tracking reporting that ecommerce-oriented or enterprise platforms build for teams that need to justify spend across multiple channels. For a solo blogger checking whether last week's newsletter landed, that's plenty. For a marketing team reporting up to stakeholders on a shared dashboard, it probably isn't enough on its own.

## So, is Kit still worth it in 2026?

For the audience this site is written for, bloggers, newsletter writers, and course creators trying to grow an audience and eventually make money from it, yes. The free plan's subscriber ceiling is generous enough to matter, the tagging system stays clean as your content grows into more topics, the automation builder does exactly what a creator-focused list needs once you're paying for it, and the deliverability reputation and migration support are real, practical advantages rather than marketing lines.

The honest caveat is that none of that comes free in the way it might first appear. You'll pay from your very first automation, not just once your list gets big, and you're choosing a tool that does one job well rather than five jobs adequately. If you want a single dashboard that also builds your website, or you're running an online store with real inventory, or your team needs CRM-grade lead scoring and branching logic, Kit isn't built for that, and forcing it into that role will leave you frustrated with a tool that was never trying to be an all-in-one platform in the first place.

If your goal is building an audience through content and eventually turning that audience into income, Kit remains one of the more purpose-built tools for the job. If your goal involves running a store, managing a sales pipeline, or replacing a whole marketing stack with one login, look elsewhere first, then come back to Kit for the email piece once you know what else you actually need.
