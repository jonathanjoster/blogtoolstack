Open Kit for the first time and it's not obvious where to start. Forms, landing pages, sequences, automations: the tool has a lot of pieces, and most guides try to explain all of them at once. That's the wrong way to learn it.

This guide does one thing. It walks you through setting up a single automated welcome sequence in Kit, from creating your account to having new subscribers automatically receive a series of emails the moment they sign up. That's the whole scope. Once this one thing works, the rest of Kit makes a lot more sense, because you'll have already touched the forms, the sequences, and the automation builder that everything else is built on.

By the end you'll have a signup form live somewhere on your blog, a short welcome sequence written and loaded into Kit, and an automation connecting the two so the whole thing runs without you touching it again.

## What you need before you start

You don't need a big list, a logo, or a fully designed blog. You need:

- A blog or website with somewhere to put a signup form. Even a sidebar or a single page is enough.
- Something to offer subscribers in exchange for their email: a free checklist, a template, a short guide, or just "new posts delivered to your inbox" if you don't have a lead magnet yet.
- About 45 minutes of focused time. Most of it goes into writing the emails, not clicking through software.

## Step 1: Create your Kit account

Go to kit.com and sign up for a free account. No credit card required. The free plan covers up to 10,000 subscribers and includes unlimited forms, landing pages, and broadcast emails, which is enough for everything in this guide except one piece.

Here's the part worth knowing upfront: the automated sequence itself, the "if someone subscribes, send these emails automatically" behavior, is a feature on Kit's paid Creator plan, not the free one. If you're not ready to pay yet, do steps 2 and 3 on the free plan and get your form live and collecting emails now. Come back for steps 4 through 6 once you're on a paid plan or trial. Kit has offered free trials of its paid tier in the past, so check the signup page for a current trial before committing to a paid plan; if one's available, it's enough time to build this whole sequence and watch it run before you decide whether to keep paying.

## Step 2: Create a signup form or landing page

In your Kit dashboard, look for the section for growing your audience. That's where forms and landing pages live. You have two options, and for a first list either one works.

A form is a smaller signup box you embed on a page you already have: a blog post, your homepage, your sidebar. Kit's form builder comes with a handful of pre-made layouts (inline, pop-up, slide-in), so you're picking a style and writing your own copy, not designing from scratch.

A landing page is a full page hosted by Kit with nothing else on it but your signup pitch. It's useful when you want one link to share on social media, in your blog's navigation, or in a bio, that's dedicated entirely to getting the signup.

For a first list, an embedded form on your homepage or your most popular post is usually the faster path to real subscribers, since it doesn't send anyone anywhere new. You can add a landing page later if you want one.

Keep the form copy short: what they get, and one line on why it's worth their email. "Get [your lead magnet], plus a short email whenever I publish something new" covers both in a single sentence.

## Step 3: Connect the form to your blog

Kit gives you a few ways to get the form live, depending on how your blog is built.

If you're on WordPress, Kit has an official plugin that lets you place forms through the block editor or as a widget, no code required. If your platform supports pasting in custom HTML or JavaScript, which covers most page builders and static site generators, Kit gives you a small embed snippet to drop wherever you want the form to show up. And if you built a landing page instead of a form in Step 2, skip embedding entirely and just link to the page's URL from your navigation, a call-to-action button, or your bio.

Publish the change, then test it yourself. Open the page in a private browser window, fill out the form with a real email address, and confirm you show up in your own Kit subscriber list a few minutes later.

## Step 4: Write a short welcome sequence

This is the step that actually takes time, and the one people try to rush. A welcome sequence is a short run of emails that go out automatically over the first week or two after someone subscribes. Three to four emails is enough for a first list. You're not writing a course. You're making a first impression and setting expectations.

A structure that works for most blogs: the first email goes out immediately and delivers whatever you promised, plus a genuine thank-you and a sentence or two on who you are and what the blog is about. Don't write your full bio here; save that for later.

The second email, sent about two days later, tells a short, specific story about why you started the blog or why the topic matters to you. This is the one that turns a stranger into someone who feels like they know you a little, which is most of what keeps people opening your emails instead of unsubscribing.

The third, sent four or five days in, points to your two or three most useful posts, the ones you'd want a brand-new reader to see first rather than just your most recent ones. It's also a natural spot to mention a tool you actually use and recommend, if that fits your blog, with a clear disclosure that it's an affiliate link.

A fourth email around the one-week mark is optional but worth adding: ask a simple question and invite a reply, like what they're struggling with or what they'd want to read next. Real replies to a welcome sequence are one of the better sources of future post ideas, and it's a habit worth starting with your very first subscriber.

Write these inside Kit's Sequences section, where each email gets its own send delay (immediately, 2 days, 5 days, and so on) set when you add it.

## Step 5: Turn on the automation

This is what makes the whole thing hands-off. In Kit's automation builder, you set up a rule: when someone subscribes through the form or landing page from Step 2, add them to the sequence you wrote in Step 4.

In practice, you create a new automation, set the trigger to something like "subscribes to a form," point it at the specific form or landing page you made, and set the resulting action to add that subscriber to your welcome sequence. Save it, turn it on, and every new subscriber starts getting your welcome emails on the schedule you set, without you lifting a finger.

Depending on how Kit's interface is laid out when you get there, there may also be a more direct route through the sequence's own settings to trigger it from a specific form, skipping the separate automation step. Either path gets you to the same place: a new signup and the first welcome email arriving a few minutes apart, automatically.

## Step 6: Test the whole thing end to end

Before calling this done, subscribe to your own form using a real inbox you check. Confirm the first welcome email lands within a few minutes. Then, and this part is easy to skip, actually wait out the 2 to 5 days to confirm the later emails arrive on schedule too, rather than assuming the delays are set correctly. Kit's automation history log shows exactly which subscribers have received which step, which is the fastest way to catch a misconfigured delay before a real reader does.

## A few mistakes worth avoiding

Don't write all four emails as pitches. If every email in the sequence asks for something, a purchase, a follow, a share, new subscribers unsubscribe fast. Keep it mostly value, with at most one soft mention of something you recommend.

Don't turn on the automation before the sequence is finished. If you activate it while emails are still missing, new subscribers hit a gap and get nothing after your first email until you catch up. Write the whole sequence first, then flip the automation on.

And don't skip checking deliverability. Test with a Gmail address and an Outlook address, not just the inbox you use every day, so you'd actually notice if your welcome emails were landing in spam. Kit's send history won't tell you that on its own.

## What's next

Once this is running, you have the real foundation of an email list: a way to collect subscribers and a sequence that greets every one of them the same way, automatically, whether you publish that day or not. From here, the natural next steps are sending regular broadcast emails to your full list and, eventually, building a second automation for a different lead magnet or topic. Neither of those requires touching what you just built.
